Federal Direct Loans · from July 1, 2026

Federal student loan limits for 2026–27

New caps for graduate students ($20,500 a year), professional students ($50,000) and parents ($20,000), and the unchanged undergraduate limits. From the final rule and the law.

$5,500–$7,500a year for dependent undergraduates, by year of study (unchanged)
$20,500a year for graduate students, $100,000 in total
$50,000a year for professional students, $200,000 in total
$20,000a year in Parent PLUS loans per student, $65,000 in total

Limits for periods of enrollment beginning on or after July 1, 2026 · final rule and law read on November 19, 2026[1],[2]

What changed on July 1, 2026

Public Law 119-21, signed on July 4, 2025, rewrote the federal loan limits for graduate students, professional students and parents. The Department of Education put the changes into its regulations with a final rule that took effect on July 1, 2026. Undergraduate limits did not change.

“These changes include establishing new loan limits for graduate students, professional students, and parents, and phasing out the Graduate PLUS (Grad PLUS) Program.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“This final rule is effective on July 1, 2026.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“A graduate student, who is not a professional student, for a period of enrollment beginning on or after July 1, 2026, may borrow up to $20,500 for any academic year under the Direct Unsubsidized Loan Program.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“A professional student, for a period of enrollment beginning on or after July 1, 2026, may borrow up to $50,000 for any academic year under the Direct Unsubsidized Loan Program.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“For periods of enrollment beginning on or after July 1, 2026, the total amount of all Direct PLUS Loans that all parents may borrow on behalf of each dependent student for any academic year of study may not exceed $20,000.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“For periods of enrollment beginning on or after July 1, 2026, the total amount of all Direct PLUS Loans that all parents may borrow on behalf of each dependent student may not exceed $65,000, without regard to any amounts repaid, forgiven, canceled, or otherwise discharged on any such loan.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“Effective July 1, 2026, the lifetime maximum aggregate amount of loans made, insured, or guaranteed under the Act that a student may borrow, shall be $257,500, excluding Federal Direct PLUS or Federal PLUS loans made to that student as a parent on behalf of another dependent undergraduate student.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]
  • The graduate and professional totals are in addition to what was borrowed for undergraduate study, as the law puts it. A student who has been both a graduate and a professional student shares one $200,000 total between the two.[2]
  • The parent limits count all parents together for each dependent student, and the $65,000 total is “without regard to any amounts repaid”: paying a loan down does not free up room to borrow again.[1]
  • The $257,500 lifetime limit is counted the same way, and loan money returned by the school or the borrower does not count against it.[1]
  • “Professional student” means a student in a program that awards a professional degree as defined in federal regulations (34 CFR 668.2). Whether a program is graduate or professional is for the school to tell you.[2]

Undergraduate limits (unchanged)

Undergraduates borrow Direct Subsidized and Direct Unsubsidized Loans. The limit depends on the year of study and on whether the student is dependent or independent for financial aid purposes. Only part of each year’s amount can be subsidized.

“The undergraduate loan limits in Sec. 685.203 remain unchanged; the aggregate Subsidized and Unsubsidized loan limits for dependent and independent undergraduates are $31,000 and $57,500, respectively.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“For example, a dependent first-year undergraduate may receive up to $5,500 in Direct Subsidized Loans and/or Direct Unsubsidized Loans for a single academic year, but no more than $3,500 of this amount may be subsidized.”

Federal Student Aid, U.S. Department of Education (FSA Partner Connect), 2025–2026 Federal Student Aid Handbook, Volume 8, Chapter 4: Annual and Aggregate Loan Limits[3]

“For example, a dependent undergraduate may have up to a maximum of $31,000 in combined subsidized and unsubsidized outstanding loan debt, but no more than $23,000 of this amount may consist of subsidized loans.”

Federal Student Aid, U.S. Department of Education (FSA Partner Connect), 2025–2026 Federal Student Aid Handbook, Volume 8, Chapter 4: Annual and Aggregate Loan Limits[3]

Four years at the dependent limits add up to $27,000 ($5,500 + $6,500 + $7,500 + $7,500; our addition), under the $31,000 total. The independent limits also apply to dependent students whose parents cannot get a Direct PLUS Loan.[3] Unpaid interest that has been added to the balance does not count toward the totals.[3]

Students who were already enrolled keep the old limits for a while

The new limits, and the end of Grad PLUS, do not apply to a student who on June 30, 2026 was enrolled in a program and had already received a federal Direct Loan for that program (or whose parent had). The exception lasts for the student’s “expected time to credential”: the time left to finish that program, and never more than 3 academic years. It ends early if the student withdraws or leaves the program.[1],[2]

What counts as the same program. For undergraduates (and their parents’ PLUS loans), changing major within the same degree or certificate at the same school counts as the same program. A graduate or professional student who switches to a different program, or any student who transfers to another school, loses the exception. A graduate student who only changes concentration within the same academic field, at the same level and school, keeps it.[1]

“a student who changes majors within the same degree or certificate shall be considered to be enrolled in the same program of study.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“Conversely, graduate students who change their program of study will be ineligible for the interim exception.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“students who change concentrations within an academic field, as long as they remain in the same 4-digit CIP code at that credential level and institution, remain eligible for the interim exception.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

“If a student transfers to a different institution, even in the same program of study, the Department would consider that to be a new program of study.”

U.S. Department of Education (Federal Register), Reimagining and Improving Student Education—Federal Student Loan Program Final Regulations; Final Rule (91 FR 23768, May 1, 2026)[1]

Two more rules that can lower your limit

Part-time enrollment. From July 1, 2026 the annual limit is reduced for students enrolled less than full time, in proportion.[2]

“the amount of a loan that student may borrow for an academic year or its equivalent shall be reduced in direct proportion to the degree to which that student is not so enrolled on a full-time basis”

U.S. Congress (U.S. Government Publishing Office), Public Law 119-21 (139 Stat. 72), sections 81001 and 82001, amending section 455 of the Higher Education Act of 1965[2]

Limits set by the school. A college may set a lower loan limit for a whole program of study, as long as it applies the same limit to every student in that program.[2] And no federal loan can exceed the school’s cost of attendance minus the student’s other financial assistance.[1]

Common questions

Questions people in the United States search for, answered from the official sources.

What are the federal student loan limits for 2026–27?

Dependent undergraduates: $5,500, $6,500 and $7,500 a year by year of study, $31,000 in total. Graduate students: $20,500 a year, $100,000 in total. Professional students: $50,000 a year, $200,000 in total. Parents: $20,000 a year and $65,000 in total for each dependent student.[1],[3]

Did the undergraduate loan limits change in 2026?

No. The final rule says the undergraduate loan limits remain unchanged: totals of $31,000 for dependent and $57,500 for independent undergraduates. The 2026 changes are for graduate students, professional students and parents.[1]

What is an aggregate loan limit?

It is the most a borrower may owe or borrow in total, on top of the yearly limit. For undergraduates it is $31,000 (dependent) or $57,500 (independent), of which at most $23,000 subsidized. From July 1, 2026 there is also a lifetime limit of $257,500 for all federal student loans.[1],[3]

Can graduate students still get a Grad PLUS loan?

Not for periods of instruction beginning on or after July 1, 2026, unless the student was enrolled in the program on June 30, 2026 and had already received a Direct Loan for it. Those students keep access for the time left in the program, up to 3 academic years. The exception is lost if the student switches to a different program or transfers to another school.[1],[2]

What did the “Big Beautiful Bill” change for student loans?

Public Law 119-21, which the Department of Education first called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and now calls the Working Families Tax Cuts Act, capped graduate loans at $20,500 a year, professional loans at $50,000 a year and Parent PLUS at $20,000 a year, ended new Grad PLUS loans, and replaced the repayment plans for new loans with a standard plan and the Repayment Assistance Plan, all from July 1, 2026.[1],[2]

How much can a graduate student borrow in federal loans?

From July 1, 2026, $20,500 a year and $100,000 in total in Direct Unsubsidized Loans, in addition to undergraduate borrowing. Students in professional degree programs can borrow $50,000 a year and $200,000 in total.[1]

Is there a lifetime limit on federal student loans?

Yes, from July 1, 2026: $257,500 for all federal student loans a person borrows as a student, counted without regard to amounts repaid or forgiven. PLUS loans taken as a parent for a child are not included.[1]

How much of my loan can be subsidized?

For undergraduates, at most $3,500 in the first year, $4,500 in the second and $5,500 from the third year on, and $23,000 in total. Graduate and professional students cannot receive subsidized loans.[3],[4]

How much can a dependent student and their parents borrow together?

Over four years, a dependent undergraduate can borrow up to $27,000 at the yearly limits (our addition), and the parents up to $65,000 in PLUS loans for that student: $92,000 in all, and never more than the cost of attendance minus other aid.[1],[3]
