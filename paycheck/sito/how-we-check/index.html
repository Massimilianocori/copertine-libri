How we check

How we check every figure

Official documents kept on file, quotations checked word for word and a second calculation that must agree to the cent.

A mistake in a paycheck calculator is a mistake about someone’s money. So every rate, threshold and table on this site is read from an official document that we download and keep, and the calculator is tested against a second, separately written calculation before the site is built.

Where the figures come from

  • We download each official document (16 files: IRS publications, state withholding booklets, state unemployment agency pages, the Department of Labor’s FUTA list) and record its address, the date and a fingerprint (SHA-256): of the whole file for PDFs and spreadsheets, of the quoted sentences for web pages, whose code changes at every download.
  • The federal tables are read from Publication 15-T, section 1: six tables of eight rows. The build stops unless the rows join up and each “tentative amount” equals the tax on the rows before it.[1]
  • California’s twelve rate tables, the low income exemption, standard deduction, estimated deduction and exemption allowance tables are read from the EDD’s Method B schedule, with the same joining-up check.[2]
  • Every sentence shown in a quotation box is copied word for word from the source and searched for in the downloaded file each time (60 sentences). If one is no longer there, the build stops.

How the calculator is tested

  • The calculator in your browser works in whole cents. A second program, written separately with exact fractions and its own reading of the official tables, computes the same paychecks; the two must agree to the cent on at least 500 random cases for each state, including pay near the Social Security wage base and the Additional Medicare Tax threshold.
  • Ten cases worked out by hand, line by line, must match.
  • The agencies’ own printed examples must come out exactly: Examples A to D of California’s Method B, the Illinois booklet’s example and the North Carolina NC-30 example.[2],[3],[4]
  • Every figure in the example tables and employer tables on the pages is recomputed and compared.

What we could not read or did not include

  • The Social Security Administration’s website refuses our checking server. We do not try to get round such blocks: the wage base comes from IRS Publication 15, which prints it.
  • This is a five-state pilot. Other states are not covered, and neither are local income taxes.
  • California’s annual and daily tables, Forms W-4 from before 2020, supplemental wages and nonresident alien rules are not in the calculator.
  • For Texas, the pages we read do not say whether a new employer pays the flat components (0.32% in all) on top of the 2.7% entry-level rate; the Texas page shows both figures and the calculator lets you type your own rate.
  • Florida’s range of rates for experienced employers is not on the page we read.

When we update

The IRS publishes the final Publication 15-T for the new year in December, and the states publish their new tables between November and January. The site moves to a new year only when the final documents are out and all the checks above pass again.

Report an error
