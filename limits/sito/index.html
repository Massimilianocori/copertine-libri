How to read a statute of limitations

  • The period is the time allowed to start the lawsuit, written in the statute (for example “within two years”).
  • The start date is when the claim “accrues”. Some statutes fix it (the date of death, the last payment); others leave it to the facts and the courts, such as when an injury was or should have been discovered.
  • Tolling pauses the clock, for example while the person with the claim is a minor or while the person being sued is out of state.
  • An outer limit (statute of repose) can bar a claim even if the injury was discovered late.
  • Claims against a government often need a written notice within months, long before the lawsuit deadline.

Recent changes some tables still miss

Negligence: from four years to two (HB 837)

Effective 24 March 2023

Chapter 2023-15, Laws of Florida, moved “an action founded on negligence” from the four-year list to the two-year list. It was approved by the Governor and took effect on March 24, 2023, and the change applies to causes of action accruing after that date. Negligence claims that accrued on or before March 24, 2023 kept the old four-year period.

Section 28. The amendments made by this act to s. 95.11, Florida Statutes, apply to causes of action accruing after the effective date of this act.

Section 31. This act shall take effect upon becoming a law.

Approved by the Governor March 24, 2023.

Laws of Florida ch. 2023-15, ss. 3, 28 and 31 · official text (Florida Department of State), read on 13 November 2026[1]

Contracts: written 8 → 6 years, oral 6 → 4 years (Senate Bill 13)

Effective 16 June 2021

The version of § 2305.06 in force until June 16, 2021 gave eight years for written contracts; § 2305.07 gave six years for contracts not in writing. Senate Bill 13 (134th General Assembly) cut them to six and four years and added the six-year rule for consumer transactions. A transition rule in that bill covers claims that accrued before June 16, 2021 (quoted below on the Ohio pages): our calculator does not compute those dates.

an action upon a specialty or an agreement, contract, or promise in writing shall be brought within eight years after the cause of action accrued.

Ohio Rev. Code § 2305.06 (version of September 28, 2012) · official text (Ohio Legislative Service Commission), read on 13 November 2026[2]

Common questions

Questions people in the United States search for, answered from the statute text. General information, not legal advice.

What does statute of limitations mean?

It is the deadline, set by law, for starting a lawsuit. The federal debt collection rule defines it as the period prescribed by applicable law for bringing a legal action. Each state sets its own periods for each kind of claim, and rules on minors, absence from the state or late discovery can move the date.[3]

What is the difference between a statute of repose and a statute of limitations?

A statute of limitations usually runs from when a claim accrues and can be paused. A statute of repose is an outer limit counted from the act itself. Texas, for example, gives health care claims two years under Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 74.251(a) and adds a 10-year cut-off that the statute calls a statute of repose, so that all claims must be brought within 10 years.[4]

What does tolling the statute of limitations mean?

Tolling means the clock stops for a while. For example, Texas does not count the time the person being sued is absent from the state, and Washington does not count the time the person with the claim is under 18 when it accrues. Each state lists its own tolling rules, and they have conditions.[5][6]

How does this statute of limitations calculator work?

Choose a state, a type of claim and the date to count from. The calculator adds the general period written in the statute and shows the date, plus any outer limit. It does not apply tolling or exceptions, does not move weekend dates and is not legal advice: it lists the exceptions so you can ask a licensed attorney.

Does the statute of limitations run against minors?

In general, many states pause or delay the deadline while the person with the claim is under 18: Texas does not count the time of the disability, and Ohio lets the claim be brought after the disability is removed. Medical malpractice often has its own shorter rules for children: in Texas a child injured before age 12 has until the 14th birthday.[5][4][7]
