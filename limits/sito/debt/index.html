Debt collection

Debt collection statute of limitations by state

General periods in ten states range from three years (Arizona, Florida, North Carolina, Virginia, Washington) to ten years (North Carolina). Statute text and exceptions for each state.

These are the periods a creditor or debt buyer has to sue to collect a debt. Many states set different periods for written contracts, oral agreements, open accounts or credit cards. A federal rule forbids debt collectors to sue or threaten to sue on a consumer debt once the period has expired. This page compares ten states (lawsuits to collect credit card balances, written contracts and open accounts).

A deadline to sue is not the end of the debt. The statutes quoted on this page limit when a lawsuit can be brought; they do not say that the debt is cancelled or erased once the period has run. The federal rule quoted on this page (Regulation F) applies to a “debt collector” as that regulation defines the term (12 CFR 1006.2(i)), so it does not cover every creditor. What appears on a credit report follows a different federal law that this site does not cover. Whether a payment or a written acknowledgment changes the deadline depends on state law; this site mentions it only where a quoted statute says so.

State by state

Texas: 4 years (debt)

(a) A person must bring suit on the following actions not later than four years after the day the cause of action accrues:

(3) debt;

Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 16.004(a)(3) · official text (Texas Legislature), read on 13 November 2026[1]

Other periods: open or stated account: 4 years (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 16.004(c)).

Florida: 5 years (written contract or obligation)

(2) WITHIN FIVE YEARS.—

(b) A legal or equitable action on a contract, obligation, or liability founded on a written instrument,

Fla. Stat. § 95.11(2)(b) · official text (Florida Legislature), read on 13 November 2026[2]

Other periods: not in writing, including store accounts: 4 years (Fla. Stat. § 95.11(3)(j)); hospital medical debt (from referral to collection): 3 years (Fla. Stat. § 95.11(4)).

Ohio: 6 years (consumer debt (personal, family or household))

an action arising out of a consumer transaction incurred primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, based upon any contract, agreement, obligation, liability, or promise, express or implied, including an account stated, whether or not reduced to writing or signed by the party to be charged by that transaction, shall be commenced within six years after the cause of action accrued.

Ohio Rev. Code § 2305.07(C) · official text (Ohio Legislative Service Commission), read on 13 November 2026[3]

Other periods: written contract (business debt): 6 years (Ohio Rev. Code § 2305.06); contract not in writing (business debt): 4 years (Ohio Rev. Code § 2305.07(A)).

Washington: 6 years (written contract)

The following actions shall be commenced within six years:

(1) An action upon a contract in writing, or liability express or implied arising out of a written agreement,

RCW 4.16.040(1) · official text (Washington State Legislature), read on 13 November 2026[4]

Other periods: account receivable: 6 years (RCW 4.16.040(2)); contract not in writing: 3 years (RCW 4.16.080(3)).

Virginia: 5 years (written and signed contract)

2. In actions on any contract that is not otherwise specified and that is in writing and signed by the party to be charged thereby, or by his agent, within five years whether such writing be under seal or not;

Va. Code § 8.01-246(A)(2) · official text (Virginia General Assembly), read on 13 November 2026[5]

Other periods: unwritten or unsigned contract: 3 years (Va. Code § 8.01-246(A)(4)); medical debt (from the final invoice due date): 3 years (Va. Code § 8.01-246(B)).

North Carolina: 3 years (contract or account)

Within three years an action -

(1) Upon a contract, obligation or liability arising out of a contract, express or implied, except those mentioned in the preceding sections or in G.S. 1-53(1).

N.C. Gen. Stat. § 1-52(1) · official text (North Carolina General Assembly), read on 13 November 2026[6]

Other periods: sealed instrument: 10 years (N.C. Gen. Stat. § 1-47(2)).

Michigan: 6 years

(9) The period of limitations is 6 years for an action to recover damages or money due for breach of contract that is not described in subsections (2) to (8).

MCL 600.5807(9) · official text (Michigan Legislature), read on 13 November 2026[7]

Arizona: 6 years (written contract signed in arizona)

A. An action for debt shall be commenced and prosecuted within six years after the cause of action accrues, and not afterward, if the indebtedness is evidenced by or founded on either of the following:

1. A contract in writing that is executed in this state.

Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 12-548(A)(1) · official text (Arizona State Legislature), read on 13 November 2026[8]

Other periods: credit card: 6 years (Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 12-548(A)(2)); debt not in writing, or open account: 3 years (Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 12-543(1)-(2)); written instrument signed outside arizona: 4 years (Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 12-544(3)).

Massachusetts: 6 years

Section 2. Actions of contract, other than those to recover for personal injuries, founded upon contracts or liabilities, express or implied, except actions limited by section one or actions upon judgments or decrees of courts of record of the United States or of this or of any other state of the United States, shall, except as otherwise provided, be commenced only within six years next after the cause of action accrues.

Mass. Gen. Laws c. 260, § 2 · official text (Massachusetts Legislature), read on 13 November 2026[9]

Minnesota: 6 years (consumer debt)

actions upon an obligation arising out of a consumer debt primarily for personal, family, or household purposes shall be commenced within six years.

Minn. Stat. § 541.053 · official text (Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes), read on 13 November 2026[10]

Other periods: other contract debt: 6 years (Minn. Stat. § 541.05, subd. 1(1)).

The federal rule on time-barred debt

Federal rule on time-barred consumer debt

Under the federal Regulation F, a debt collector must not sue or threaten to sue a consumer over a debt once the applicable statute of limitations has expired.

A debt collector must not bring or threaten to bring a legal action against a consumer to collect a time-barred debt.

12 CFR 1006.26(b) · official text (eCFR, Office of the Federal Register), read on 13 November 2026[11]

Common questions

Questions people in the United States search for, answered from the statute text. General information, not legal advice.

Can a debt collector sue after the statute of limitations?

Under the federal Regulation F, a debt collector must not bring or threaten to bring a legal action against a consumer to collect a time-barred debt, meaning a debt for which the applicable statute of limitations has expired. Which period applies, and whether it has expired, depends on state law and the facts.[11]

What is a time-barred debt?

The federal rule defines a time-barred debt as a debt for which the applicable statute of limitations has expired. The debt may still exist, but a debt collector must not sue or threaten to sue the consumer over it.[11]

What is the statute of limitations for debt collection by state?

Among the ten states on this site: Texas four years, Florida five years for written obligations and four years for those not in writing, Ohio six years for consumer debt, Washington six years for written contracts, Virginia five years for signed written contracts, North Carolina three years, Michigan six years, Arizona six years for written contracts and credit cards, Massachusetts six years and Minnesota six years.[1][2][3][10]
