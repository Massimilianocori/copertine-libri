What the Minnesota statute says

Minnesota sets more than one period for personal injury claims, depending on the kind of claim:

  • Negligence and other injury to the person 6 years

    Except where the Uniform Commercial Code otherwise prescribes, the following actions shall be commenced within six years:

    (5) for criminal conversation, or for any other injury to the person or rights of another, not arising on contract, and not hereinafter enumerated;

    Minn. Stat. § 541.05, subd. 1(5) · official text (Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes), read on 13 November 2026[1]

  • Assault, battery and other intentional torts 2 years

    the following actions shall be commenced within two years:

    (1) for libel, slander, assault, battery, false imprisonment, or other tort resulting in personal injury,

    Minn. Stat. § 541.07(1) · official text (Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes), read on 13 November 2026[2]

Minnesota has two possible periods for injuries. Section 541.07(1) gives two years for libel, slander, assault, battery, false imprisonment “or other tort resulting in personal injury”, while § 541.05 gives six years for “any other injury to the person”. Negligence claims are generally treated under the six-year period; some tables online show two years for all injuries. Ask a Minnesota attorney which applies to your claim.

When the clock starts

From the day the claim accrues; the sections do not define it.

For example, counted from 15 January 2025, the general deadline would be Negligence and other injury to the person: 15 January 2031; Assault, battery and other intentional torts: 15 January 2027. Exceptions below can move it.

Exceptions and special rules for personal injury

Product strict liability: four years

A claim based on the strict liability of the defendant and arising from the manufacture, sale, use or consumption of a product has four years.

any action based on the strict liability of the defendant and arising from the manufacture, sale, use or consumption of a product shall be commenced within four years.

Minn. Stat. § 541.05, subd. 2 · official text (Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes), read on 13 November 2026[1]

Rules that pause or extend deadlines in Minnesota

These general rules apply to many Minnesota deadlines, including this one, subject to their own conditions.

Minors and insanity (with limits)

Being under 18 or insane suspends the period until the disability is removed; except for infancy, the extension cannot exceed five years, and never more than one year after the disability ends.

provided that such period, except in the case of infancy, shall not be extended for more than five years, nor in any case for more than one year after the disability ceases:

Minn. Stat. § 541.15(a) · official text (Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes), read on 13 November 2026[3]

Defendant out of state

Time while the person being sued is out of state and cannot be served is not counted.

the time of the person's absence is not part of the time limited for the commencement of the action.

Minn. Stat. § 541.13 · official text (Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes), read on 13 November 2026[4]

If the claim is against a government

Claims against cities and other municipalities: 180-day notice

Notice must be presented to the municipality within 180 days after the loss or injury is discovered (one year for a wrongful death claim).

shall cause to be presented to the governing body of the municipality within 180 days after the alleged loss or injury is discovered a notice stating the time, place and circumstances thereof,

Minn. Stat. § 466.05 · official text (Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes), read on 13 November 2026[5]

What is the statute of limitations for personal injury in Minnesota?

Minnesota has two possible periods: six years under Minn. Stat. § 541.05, subd. 1(5) for any other injury to the person, which is generally used for negligence, and two years under Minn. Stat. § 541.07(1) for libel, slander, assault, battery, false imprisonment or other tort resulting in personal injury. Ask a Minnesota attorney which applies.[1][2]
